SLF Standard 4: Culture of Learning and Positive Behavior

Standard 4 addresses how principals shape the culture of their schools — for both educators and students. Culture is not a byproduct of school improvement; it is a precondition for it. Two components organize this standard: 4A (Creates or maintains a culture conducive to educator growth) and 4B (Creates or maintains a culture conducive to student growth). Together they describe the conditions that allow instruction and learning to occur at the highest level.

Component 4A is closely related to Standard 3 — the coaching, collaboration, and feedback structures described in Standard 3 only work if the principal has built the trust, risk tolerance, and professional norms described in 4A. Component 4B encompasses the school's equity commitments in practice: how the school addresses bias, promotes cultural competence, responds to behavior, and creates the emotional safety students need to take academic risks.

Component 4A: Creates or Maintains a Culture Conducive to Educator Growth

This component describes the professional culture a principal cultivates among adults in the building — the norms of trust, risk-taking, courageous conversation, and equity that allow coaching and professional growth to take root. Without the culture described in 4A, the coaching and feedback structures described in Standard 3 will not produce the growth they are designed to achieve. Culture is the soil; Standard 3 is the seed.

4A.1
Builds Trust to Promote a Culture of Risk-Taking and Learning
Creates a culture where most staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks, try new practices, and learn from mistakes — a prerequisite for meaningful professional growth and instructional change.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Creates a culture where most staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks and try new practices.
Most staff articulate that they can make mistakes without fear of punitive action and can request assistance without jeopardizing their evaluation.
Builds, or has in place, a school-wide system of trust and support for innovation; most staff report feeling trusted and supported.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically builds a culture where all staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks; nearly all staff articulate that they feel trusted, supported, and inspired to take risks and try new practices; innovation is recognized and celebrated.
No evidence for Building Trust to Promote a Culture of Risk-Taking and Learning observed.
Connection to Standard 3: The quality of coaching conversations (3C.2) depends on the trust established in 4A.1. A principal can have excellent observation skills and give accurate, specific feedback — but if staff do not feel safe enough to receive and act on that feedback without fear, the coaching loop fails. Supervisors should look for whether staff speak openly about their struggles and actively seek feedback, which signals a trust culture the principal has built.
Examples of Evidence
School Experience Survey data showing staff report feeling trusted and supported
Observation of staff interactions — do they speak openly about challenges and ask for help?
Staff voluntarily share student work, lesson plans, or observation invitations
Evidence of principal modeling vulnerability or admitting mistakes publicly
Norms for risk-taking and learning from failure incorporated into PD or meeting culture
Recognition structures that celebrate learning and innovation, not just outcomes
Ineffective
Does not build a culture of trust; most staff feel neither trusted nor supported enough to take risks or try new practices, or feel that mistakes will be punished. Staff are reluctant to request or receive assistance.
Developing
Inconsistently builds a culture of trust; some staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks and try new practices. Some staff articulate that they can make mistakes without fear of punitive action.
Effective
Creates a culture where most staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks and try new practices; most staff articulate that they can make mistakes without fear of punitive action and can request assistance without jeopardizing their evaluation. Builds or has in place a school-wide system of trust and support for innovation.
Highly Effective
Systematically builds a culture where all staff feel trusted and supported enough to take risks and try new practices; nearly all staff articulate that they feel trusted, supported, and inspired to take risks and try new practices. Innovation is recognized and celebrated.
4A.2
Engages in and Facilitates Courageous Conversations About Inequities
Regularly engages staff in honest, data-grounded conversations about inequitable practices and outcomes; most staff are able to identify and discuss inequities in instruction, discipline, access, or opportunity and commit to addressing them.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Regularly engages staff in honest conversations about inequitable practices and outcomes, using data to ground the conversation.
Most staff are able to identify and discuss inequities and commit to taking action to address them.
Courageous conversations address data disparities — including disproportionate discipline, access to rigorous content, EL reclassification rates, and gifted identification rates.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically creates opportunities for all staff to engage in honest conversations; nearly all staff initiate and sustain courageous conversations about inequity as part of the school's ongoing professional culture.
No evidence for Engaging in and Facilitating Courageous Conversations About Inequities observed.
Site visit connection: During leadership visits, the quality of the debrief conversation is evidence for this element. Does the principal name inequitable patterns observed in classrooms — disproportionate teacher attention, low cognitive demand for specific student groups, or EL students not accessing rigorous content — or do they focus only on what went well? A principal comfortable with courageous conversation will name what they saw without hedging.
Examples of Evidence
Agendas, notes, or protocols from meetings in which staff discuss inequitable practices or outcomes
Disaggregated data by race, ethnicity, language, and disability status used in staff discussions
Data showing disproportionate discipline, access, and achievement gaps addressed in faculty discourse
Staff surveys indicating comfort with discussing equity and race
Principal models naming inequity in public settings (faculty meetings, PD, debrief conversations)
Action plans responding to identified inequities with specific, measurable commitments
Ineffective
Avoids or rarely initiates conversations about inequitable practices or outcomes; staff are not encouraged or supported to identify or discuss inequities; inequitable practices or outcomes continue without examination or response.
Developing
Occasionally engages staff in conversations about inequitable practices or outcomes; some staff are able to identify inequities but may not commit to taking action to address them.
Effective
Regularly engages staff in honest conversations about inequitable practices and outcomes, using data to ground the conversation; most staff are able to identify and discuss inequities and commit to taking action to address them.
Highly Effective
Systematically creates opportunities for all staff to engage in honest conversations about inequitable practices and outcomes; nearly all staff initiate and sustain courageous conversations about inequity and consistently take action to address inequitable practices and outcomes.
4A.3
Promotes Educator Cultural Competence and Culturally Responsive Practices
Consistently promotes and models cultural competence; most staff regularly demonstrate culturally responsive practices in the classroom and in professional settings — in curriculum, instruction, communication, and relationship-building.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Consistently promotes and models cultural competence among staff.
Most staff regularly demonstrate culturally responsive practices in their classrooms and in professional settings.
Culturally responsive practices are evident in curriculum selection, instructional strategy, communication with families, and relationship-building with students.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically promotes and models cultural competence; nearly all staff demonstrate culturally responsive practices; school-wide systems embed CLRP across all academic and non-academic settings.
No evidence for Promoting Educator Cultural Competence and Culturally Responsive Practices observed.
Examples of Evidence
PD agendas and artifacts addressing CLRP — culturally and linguistically responsive pedagogy
Classroom observations showing culturally affirming instructional strategies
Curriculum materials that reflect the backgrounds, languages, and identities of students
Staff and student surveys on sense of belonging and cultural affirmation
School leader models culturally responsive communication in public settings
Evidence of multilingual resources and culturally relevant content throughout school environments
CLRP referenced in hiring, coaching, and evaluation conversations
Ineffective
Does not promote cultural competence among staff; few staff demonstrate culturally responsive practices in their classrooms or in professional settings; culturally responsive practices are not embedded in curriculum, instruction, or communication.
Developing
Inconsistently promotes cultural competence; some staff demonstrate culturally responsive practices in their classrooms or professional settings; culturally responsive practices are embedded in some curriculum, instruction, or communication.
Effective
Consistently promotes and models cultural competence among staff; most staff regularly demonstrate culturally responsive practices in their classrooms and in professional settings; culturally responsive practices are evident in curriculum, instruction, communication, and relationship-building.
Highly Effective
Systematically promotes and models cultural competence among all staff; nearly all staff consistently demonstrate culturally responsive practices in their classrooms and professional settings; culturally responsive practices are embedded throughout all aspects of the school's academic and non-academic programming.

Component 4B: Creates or Maintains a Culture Conducive to Student Growth

This component addresses how principals shape the student-facing culture of the school — the systems, expectations, and relationships that determine whether students feel safe, valued, motivated, and able to take the academic and social-emotional risks that lead to growth. Component 4B connects directly to what region leadership observes in classrooms and hallways during school site visits: the quality of student-adult relationships, the consistency of behavioral expectations, and whether students experience a sense of belonging and high expectation simultaneously.

4B.1
Promotes High Expectations for All Students
Consistently communicates and promotes a school-wide culture of high expectations for the academic achievement and personal growth of all students — including those traditionally underserved — and challenges practices that hold lower expectations for any student group.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Consistently communicates and promotes high expectations for all students' academic achievement and personal growth.
Challenges practices that communicate or reflect lower expectations for any student group — including ELs, students with disabilities, and students experiencing poverty.
Most staff reflect and enact high expectations in their instruction, feedback, and relationship with students.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically communicates and promotes high expectations; nearly all staff reflect and enact high expectations; the school culture itself communicates that every student is capable of rigorous academic achievement.
No evidence for Promoting High Expectations for All Students observed.
Instructional leadership connection: When TLF element 2b2 (Expectations for Learning and Achievement) is observed during site visits, what is seen in classrooms also reflects the principal's 4B.1 practice. A school where some students — often ELs or students with disabilities — are consistently assigned lower-complexity tasks or receive less frequent academic press from their teachers is a school where the principal has not yet established the culture of high expectations this element describes.
Examples of Evidence
Enrollment data showing equitable access to rigorous coursework (A-G, AP, honors) for all subgroups
Observation of school leader publicly affirming high expectations in faculty meetings, assemblies, and student settings
Classroom observations showing high cognitive demand maintained for all students
School leader challenges teachers whose feedback or grouping communicates lower expectations
Student recognition systems that celebrate academic growth across all learner profiles
Reduction in tracking or ability grouping that limits access to grade-level content
Ineffective
Does not consistently communicate or promote high expectations for all students; allows or models practices that communicate lower expectations for some student groups; few staff consistently reflect high expectations in their instruction or relationships with students.
Developing
Inconsistently communicates or promotes high expectations for all students; some staff reflect high expectations in their instruction or relationships with students; lower expectations for some student groups may persist without challenge.
Effective
Consistently communicates and promotes a school-wide culture of high expectations for all students' academic achievement and personal growth; challenges practices that communicate or reflect lower expectations for any student group; most staff reflect and enact high expectations in their instruction, feedback, and relationship with students.
Highly Effective
Systematically communicates and promotes a school-wide culture of high expectations; actively dismantles practices that communicate lower expectations; nearly all staff consistently reflect and enact high expectations; the school culture communicates that every student is capable of rigorous academic achievement and personal growth.
4B.2
Promotes Student Motivation, Engagement, and Love of Learning
Creates and maintains systems and structures that cultivate students' intrinsic motivation, engagement, and curiosity — where most students are actively engaged in meaningful work and experience a sense of ownership over their learning.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Creates or maintains systems and structures that cultivate student intrinsic motivation and engagement.
Most students are actively engaged in meaningful, relevant work and experience a sense of ownership over their learning.
Students can articulate what they are learning and why it matters to them.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically creates and sustains structures for intrinsic motivation; nearly all students are genuinely engaged; student voice and agency are embedded in school structures and decision-making.
No evidence for Promoting Student Motivation, Engagement, and Love of Learning observed.
Examples of Evidence
Student Work Exhibits and presentations visible throughout the school
Student surveys indicating engagement, sense of belonging, and interest in learning
Students can articulate the purpose and relevance of their work when asked
Project-based learning, student choice, and authentic audiences embedded in instructional design
Student attendance and participation data showing school-wide trends over time
Student voice structures — advisory committees, student government with genuine decision-making power
Ineffective
Does not create systems or structures to cultivate student motivation or engagement; few students are actively engaged in meaningful work; students rarely demonstrate ownership over their learning or interest in the content.
Developing
Inconsistently creates systems and structures that cultivate student motivation; some students are actively engaged in meaningful work and demonstrate some ownership over their learning.
Effective
Creates or maintains systems and structures that cultivate student intrinsic motivation and engagement; most students are actively engaged in meaningful, relevant work and experience a sense of ownership over their learning; students can articulate what they are learning and why it matters.
Highly Effective
Systematically creates and sustains structures for student intrinsic motivation; nearly all students are genuinely engaged; student voice and agency are embedded in school-wide structures and decision-making; students regularly demonstrate curiosity, persistence, and love of learning across settings.
4B.3
Implements Equitable Discipline Practices That Keep Students in School
Implements discipline policies and practices that are equitable, restorative, and minimally exclusionary — reducing suspension and expulsion rates while maintaining a safe and productive learning environment; disproportionate discipline by race, language, or disability status is actively monitored and addressed.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Implements discipline policies and practices that are equitable and minimally exclusionary, prioritizing restorative and preventive approaches.
Suspension and expulsion rates are monitored and actively reduced; disproportionate discipline by student subgroup is identified and addressed.
Most students who engage in challenging behavior are supported and returned to productive participation in school as quickly as possible.
At the Highly Effective level: Disproportionate discipline data by subgroup has been systematically reduced; restorative practices are embedded school-wide; nearly all students remain in school even following significant behavioral incidents.
No evidence for Implementing Equitable Discipline Practices observed.
Examples of Evidence
Discipline data disaggregated by race, ethnicity, language, disability status — trend analysis over time
Evidence of restorative justice practices, counseling, and re-entry protocols
Suspension and expulsion rates compared to district averages and prior years
Positive Behavior Intervention and Supports (PBIS) implementation data
Reduction in office referrals as preventive classroom management supports are implemented
Staff training logs for trauma-informed and restorative practices
Ineffective
Implements discipline policies that are exclusionary, inconsistent, or disproportionately applied to some student groups; suspension and expulsion rates are high or increasing; students who engage in challenging behavior are removed from school without restorative alternatives.
Developing
Attempts to implement equitable discipline practices, but with inconsistent results; some exclusionary discipline persists; disproportionate discipline by subgroup may be acknowledged but not yet systematically addressed.
Effective
Implements discipline policies and practices that are equitable and minimally exclusionary; suspension and expulsion rates are monitored and actively reduced; disproportionate discipline by subgroup is identified and addressed; most students who engage in challenging behavior are supported and returned to productive participation in school.
Highly Effective
Disproportionate discipline by student subgroup has been systematically reduced through proactive, restorative, and preventive practices embedded school-wide; nearly all students remain engaged in school even following significant behavioral incidents; the school community demonstrates shared commitment to keeping students in school.
4B.4
Promotes Student Social-Emotional Learning and Well-Being
Creates and maintains systems and structures that support students' social-emotional learning and well-being — including trauma-informed practices, mental health supports, and the four LAUSD SEL competencies: Growth Mindset, Self-Efficacy, Self-Management, and Social Awareness.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Creates or maintains school-wide systems and structures that actively support students' social-emotional learning and well-being.
Trauma-informed practices are embedded in classroom and school culture — not limited to crisis response.
Most students demonstrate and can articulate the LAUSD SEL competencies: Growth Mindset, Self-Efficacy, Self-Management, and Social Awareness.
Mental health and counseling supports are accessible and proactively connected to students in need.
At the Highly Effective level: SEL competencies are systematically embedded across academic and non-academic settings; nearly all students demonstrate SEL competencies; staff receive ongoing training and model SEL practices themselves.
No evidence for Promoting Student Social-Emotional Learning and Well-Being observed.
LAUSD SEL Competencies: Growth Mindset — the belief that one's abilities can grow with effort. Self-Efficacy — the belief in one's ability to succeed. Self-Management — the ability to regulate emotions, thoughts, and behaviors effectively. Social Awareness — the ability to take the perspective of others from diverse backgrounds and understand social and ethical norms for behavior.
Examples of Evidence
School-wide SEL program implementation data and artifacts
Student surveys on sense of belonging, safety, and emotional support
Evidence of trauma-informed practices in classroom and hallway interactions
Counseling and mental health referral data — access and timeliness
Staff PD on SEL competencies and trauma-informed instruction
Students can articulate growth mindset language and self-management strategies
Reduction in chronic absenteeism linked to SEL and belonging interventions
Ineffective
Does not create systems or structures to support students' social-emotional learning or well-being; trauma-informed practices are absent; SEL competencies are not referenced or taught; students in need lack access to mental health supports.
Developing
Inconsistently creates systems and structures to support student SEL; some trauma-informed practices are in place; SEL competencies are occasionally referenced; some students can access mental health supports when needed.
Effective
Creates or maintains school-wide systems and structures that support students' SEL and well-being; trauma-informed practices are embedded in classroom and school culture; most students demonstrate the LAUSD SEL competencies; mental health and counseling supports are accessible and proactively connected to students in need.
Highly Effective
SEL competencies are systematically embedded across all academic and non-academic settings; nearly all students demonstrate SEL competencies and can articulate them; staff receive ongoing training and consistently model SEL practices; trauma-informed practices are a defining feature of the school's culture and systems.
