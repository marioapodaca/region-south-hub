Standard 2: The Classroom Environment

This reference covers all four components of TLF Standard 2 — the standard most directly observable during classroom walkthroughs and informal visits. Standard 2 describes the conditions that make instruction possible: the relationships, culture, procedures, and behavioral structures that allow students to take risks and engage in learning. Use this page alongside Standard 3 (Delivery of Instruction) when conducting informal observations and classroom visits.

Component 2a: Creating an Environment of Respect and Rapport

Teaching depends, fundamentally, on the quality of relationships among individuals. When teachers strive to engage students in a discussion or an activity, their interactions speak volumes about the extent to which they value students as people and teach them to manage their thoughts and behaviors, empathize with, and understand the perspective of others.

2a1
Teacher Interaction with Students
Teacher interacts with students in a way that projects respect and creates a safe and supportive learning environment. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Teacher interactions are friendly and demonstrate caring and respect for the class as a whole.
Interactions are appropriate to the age and cultures of the students.
Student self-management and self-efficacy are consistently nurtured.
Students exhibit respect for the teacher.
At the Highly Effective level: Teacher's interactions reflect respect, caring, and cultural understanding for individuals as well as groups. Students appear to trust the teacher with sensitive information and demonstrate mutual respect across a variety of contexts.
No evidence for Teacher Interaction with Students observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Greeting students by name; using culturally responsive and affirming language; acknowledging student identity, language, and background; maintaining appropriate tone and register at all times; avoiding sarcasm, put-downs, or demeaning responses to student errors; modeling self-management and social awareness. Responding to the teacher respectfully; engaging willingly without fear of ridicule; demonstrating self-management in interactions with the teacher and peers; at the Highly Effective level, students trust the teacher enough to share sensitive information and initiate open dialogue.
Ineffective
Teacher interaction with at least some students is negative, demeaning, sarcastic, or inappropriate to the age or culture of the students. Student self-management and self-efficacy are not nurtured. Students exhibit disrespect for the teacher.
Developing
Teacher-student interactions are generally appropriate but may reflect occasional inconsistencies, favoritism, or disregard for students' cultures. Student self-management and self-efficacy are inconsistently nurtured. Students exhibit only minimal respect for the teacher.
Effective
Teacher-student interactions are friendly and demonstrate caring and respect for the class as a whole. Such interactions are appropriate to the age and cultures of the students. Student self-management and self-efficacy are consistently nurtured. Students exhibit respect for the teacher.
Highly Effective
Teacher's interactions with students reflect respect, caring, and cultural understanding for individuals as well as groups of students. Students demonstrate self-management and self-efficacy and appear to trust the teacher with sensitive information and have a mutual respect and open dialogue in a variety of contexts.
2a2
Student Interactions with One Another
Students interact with one another in a respectful, polite manner that promotes a positive learning environment for all students. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Student interactions are consistently polite, respectful, and demonstrate empathy for others.
Students engage with peers without conflict, sarcasm, or put-downs.
At the Highly Effective level: Students demonstrate empathy by caring for one another, monitoring peers' treatment of classmates, and correcting peers respectfully when needed. Students assume personal responsibility and self-management.
No evidence for Student Interactions with One Another observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Establishing and reinforcing norms for peer interaction; explicitly teaching and modeling empathy, active listening, and respectful disagreement; addressing disrespectful peer interactions promptly and constructively; structuring collaborative activities that promote positive student-to-student relationships. Listening attentively to peers; responding with respect even when disagreeing; refraining from put-downs, sarcasm, or dismissive behavior; at the Highly Effective level, actively monitoring and addressing disrespectful peer interactions independently and assuming personal responsibility for the classroom community.
Ineffective
Student interactions are characterized by conflict, sarcasm, or put-downs, or lack of empathy.
Developing
Student interactions are usually appropriate but may reflect occasional displays of disrespect or a lack of empathy from a few students.
Effective
Student interactions are consistently polite, respectful, and demonstrate empathy for others.
Highly Effective
Students demonstrate empathy by caring for one another and monitoring one another's treatment of peers, correcting classmates respectfully when needed, and assuming and demonstrating personal responsibility and self-management.
2a3
Academic Climate
The classroom environment is safe and supportive; risk-taking is encouraged, students freely contribute their ideas, and student mistakes are treated as learning opportunities, never with ridicule. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO). EDST Focus Element
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Students demonstrate a growth mindset by freely sharing their ideas or opinions and taking risks in learning.
Student mistakes are treated as learning opportunities by the teacher.
Students volunteer to share even when they are uncertain — they are not afraid to be wrong.
At the Highly Effective level: Both student and teacher mistakes are treated as learning opportunities. Students and teacher share academic struggles openly.
No evidence for Academic Climate look-fors observed.
EDST Focus Element — Academic Climate (2a3) is a focus element for the EDST evaluation cycle. The key question is whether students feel psychologically safe enough to take academic risks. An environment where only certain students respond, or where wrong answers produce embarrassment, does not meet Effective level.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Responding to incorrect answers with curiosity and encouragement rather than correction or dismissal; publicly normalizing mistakes as part of learning; praising effort and process over performance; consistently inviting all students — not just confident or high-performing ones — to contribute ideas. Volunteering answers and ideas even when unsure; using language of growth mindset; engaging with challenging tasks without visible anxiety; at the Highly Effective level, students and teacher together name and learn from mistakes — including teacher errors — as a shared norm of the classroom.
Ineffective
Students do not freely share their ideas or opinions or demonstrate a growth mindset. Student mistakes may be ridiculed by the teacher or other students.
Developing
Some students demonstrate a growth mindset and freely share their ideas or opinions. Risk-taking and mistakes receive unpredictable responses from the teacher or other students.
Effective
Students demonstrate a growth mindset by freely sharing their ideas or opinions and taking risks in learning. Student mistakes are treated as learning opportunities by the teacher.
Highly Effective
Students demonstrate a growth mindset by freely sharing their ideas, opinions, or academic struggles and taking risks in learning. Student and teacher mistakes are treated as learning opportunities by the teacher and students.

Component 2b: Establishing a Culture for Learning

A "culture for learning" refers to the atmosphere in the classroom that reflects the importance of the work undertaken by both students and teacher. It describes the norms that govern interactions about activities and assignments, the look of the classroom, and the general "tone" of the class. A culture for learning implies high expectations for all students, ensuring classrooms are cognitively busy places that nurture and support a growth mindset and self-efficacy.

2b1
Importance of the Content
Teacher links content to students' lives, essential questions, or long-term goals. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Teacher conveys enthusiasm for the content, and students value the importance of the content.
Teacher links content to students' lives, essential questions, or long-term goals.
At the Highly Effective level: Students demonstrate through active participation, curiosity, and taking initiative that they value the content's importance. Teacher and students link content to real-world applications, essential questions, and long-range goals. Content is made culturally relevant and applicable to students' lives.
No evidence for Importance of the Content observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Explicitly connecting the day's content to students' identities, communities, or future goals; framing content through essential questions that resonate personally; expressing genuine enthusiasm for the subject matter; drawing culturally relevant connections; positioning the content as meaningful — not just mandated. Showing engagement with the content beyond compliance — asking questions, making connections, volunteering additional ideas; at the Highly Effective level, students initiate connections to their own lives and prior knowledge, demonstrating internalized value for the content rather than teacher-prompted engagement.
Ineffective
Teacher conveys a negative attitude toward the content, suggesting it is not important or has been mandated by others. Teacher makes no attempt to link content to students' lives or essential questions.
Developing
Teacher communicates importance of the work but with little conviction or with minimal links to real-world application. Only some students value the importance of the content.
Effective
Teacher conveys enthusiasm for the content, and students value the importance of the content. Teacher links content to students' lives, essential questions, or long-term goals.
Highly Effective
Students demonstrate through their active participation, curiosity, and taking initiative that they value the content's importance. Teacher and students link content to real-world applications, essential questions, and long-range goals. Teacher and students make content culturally relevant and applicable to students' lives.
2b2
Expectations for Learning and Achievement
The culture of the classroom reflects clear and high expectations about what is expected of students in order for them to learn and achieve. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
The classroom environment conveys a culture of high expectations for all students.
Instructional outcomes, activities, assignments, and classroom interactions convey high expectations.
Teacher demonstrates clear and high expectations about what is expected of students — including effort.
Overall learning expectations are clear to all students and consistently related to standards.
At the Highly Effective level: Students appear to have internalized these expectations. Students set their own goals and monitor their own progress toward achieving mastery of standards. There is a celebration of growth and achieving personal bests.
No evidence for Expectations for Learning and Achievement observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Communicating learning objectives clearly; holding all students — including historically underserved subgroups — to the same high academic standard; assigning tasks with appropriate cognitive demand; communicating expectations for quality of work and effort explicitly; not accepting low-quality work without feedback and an opportunity to revise. Attempting challenging tasks without giving up; asking for help or clarification rather than disengaging; working toward clear learning objectives; at the Highly Effective level, students set personal learning goals, track their own progress, and celebrate growth — not just performance.
Ineffective
The classroom environment conveys a culture of low expectations. Instructional outcomes, activities, assignments, and classroom interactions lack challenge and support. Overall learning expectations are not made clear to students or are not linked to standards.
Developing
The classroom environment conveys a culture of modest expectations. Teacher demonstrates uneven expectations regarding which students can learn and achieve. Overall learning expectations are either unclear to students or inconsistently related to standards.
Effective
The classroom environment conveys a culture of high expectations. Instructional outcomes, activities, assignments, and classroom interactions convey high expectations for all students. Overall learning expectations are clear to all students and consistently related to standards.
Highly Effective
Students appear to have internalized high expectations. Students set their own goals and monitor their own progress toward achieving mastery of standards. There is a celebration of growth and achieving personal bests for students and the teacher. Overall learning expectations are clear to all students and consistently related to standards.
2b3
Student Ownership of Their Work
Students demonstrate ownership of their work and share their learning with others. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Students accept responsibility for doing high-quality work and demonstrate ownership of the assigned work.
Students are invested in the quality of their work — not just completion.
At the Highly Effective level: Students demonstrate attention to detail and take obvious ownership of their work, initiating improvements — for example, revising drafts on their own or helping peers.
No evidence for Student Ownership of Their Work observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Designing tasks that students find meaningful and feel proud of completing; communicating clear quality criteria so students know what "done well" looks like; providing opportunities for students to share, present, and receive feedback on their work; responding to low-quality work with feedback and a path to improvement rather than just a grade. Taking care with their work product — not rushing to finish; re-reading, revising, and improving work without being told to; expressing interest in how their peers approach the same task; at the Highly Effective level, students independently initiate revisions and peer support, demonstrating intrinsic investment in quality.
Ineffective
Students demonstrate no ownership of their work. Students are uninterested in their work.
Developing
Students demonstrate little ownership of their work. Completion of the task is more important than the quality of the work.
Effective
Students accept responsibility for doing high-quality work and demonstrate ownership of the assigned work.
Highly Effective
Students demonstrate attention to detail and take obvious ownership of their work, initiating improvements in it by, for example, revising drafts on their own or helping peers.
2b4
Physical Environment
The classroom is designed to ensure safety and promote student learning. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
The classroom is safe and organized to support student learning and collaboration.
Teacher uses the physical environment to provide access to instructional resources and display evidence of student learning.
At the Highly Effective level: Both teacher and students use the physical environment purposefully to access instructional resources and display evidence of student learning.
No evidence for the Physical Environment look-fors observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Arranging seating to support the instructional goals of the lesson (collaborative, whole-group, stations); displaying anchor charts, vocabulary, student work, and learning targets in accessible, purposeful locations; ensuring materials are organized and accessible; maintaining a physically safe and welcoming environment. Using the physical environment as a resource — referring to posted vocabulary, anchor charts, or criteria for success; navigating the space efficiently to access materials; at the Highly Effective level, students take ownership of the environment — contributing to displays, maintaining organization, and using resources independently and purposefully.
Ineffective
The classroom is unsafe, or the physical environment impedes student learning. Teacher makes little or no use of resources to support student learning or collaboration.
Developing
The classroom is safe but not organized to support student learning and collaboration. The teacher occasionally uses the physical environment, but with limited effect on student learning or collaboration.
Effective
The classroom is safe and organized to support student learning and collaboration. The teacher uses the physical environment to provide access to instructional resources and display evidence of student learning.
Highly Effective
The classroom is safe and organized to support student learning and collaboration. Both teacher and students use the physical environment purposefully to access instructional resources and display evidence of student learning.

Component 2c: Managing Classroom Procedures

A smoothly functioning classroom is a prerequisite to good instruction. Teachers must develop procedures for the smooth operation of the classroom and the efficient use of time. One of the marks of effective teachers is that they can take the time required to establish routines and procedures at the outset of the school year. Effective teachers make efficient use of time when managing transitions and non-instructional tasks.

2c1
Management of Routines, Procedures, and Transitions
Routines, procedures, and transitions are managed in such a way that there is little to no loss of instructional time. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO). EDST Focus Element
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Transitions occur with little loss of instructional time.
Teacher prompts students to use efficient routines with minimal direction.
At the Highly Effective level: Transitions occur with little to no loss of instructional time. Students initiate and efficiently use routines and procedures appropriate to the task, transition, or grouping structure — without being told.
No evidence for Management of Routines, Procedures, and Transitions observed.
EDST Focus Element — Management of Routines, Procedures, and Transitions (2c1) is a focus element for the EDST evaluation cycle. Transitions are one of the clearest windows into how much instructional time is protected and how well students have internalized classroom expectations.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Giving clear signals for transitions (verbal, visual, or timed); using established routines so students know what to do without repeated explanation; minimizing time between activity segments; monitoring the pace of transitions and adjusting as needed; having materials ready before the lesson begins. Moving efficiently between activities with minimal prompting; following established routines without confusion or waiting; at the Highly Effective level, students independently initiate and execute transitions, freeing the teacher to circulate and provide feedback.
Ineffective
Transitions result in much time being lost between activities or lesson segments. Students do not appear to know what is expected for specific routines or transitions.
Developing
Some transitions result in loss of instructional time. Students require specific direction and oversight from the teacher in order to execute routines and transitions.
Effective
Transitions occur with little loss of instructional time. Teacher prompts students to use efficient routines with minimal direction.
Highly Effective
Transitions occur with little to no loss of instructional time. Students initiate and efficiently use routines and procedures appropriate to the task, transition, or grouping structure.
2c2
Management of Materials and Supplies
Materials and supplies are distributed/available to students in a way that does not interrupt instructional time. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Materials are prepared and gathered in advance of the lesson.
Routines for handling materials and supplies function smoothly with little loss of instructional time.
Students assume responsibility for materials when directed by the teacher.
At the Highly Effective level: Routines for handling materials are seamless, with students assuming some responsibility for smooth operation without teacher direction.
No evidence for Management of Materials and Supplies observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Pre-staging materials at tables or stations before students arrive; using established systems for distributing and collecting materials; not stopping instruction to gather or locate supplies; having technology charged and ready. Retrieving and returning materials efficiently using established procedures; assuming assigned roles without disruption; at the Highly Effective level, students take initiative with materials management — keeping the flow of the lesson smooth without teacher prompting.
Ineffective
Materials and supplies are handled inefficiently or have not been prepared in advance, resulting in significant loss of instructional time.
Developing
Some materials are prepared in advance, but there is some loss of instructional time while the teacher accesses or gathers materials or supplies. Routines function moderately well but with some loss of time.
Effective
Materials are prepared and gathered in advance of the lesson. Routines for handling materials and supplies occur smoothly, with little loss of instructional time. Students assume responsibility when directed by the teacher.
Highly Effective
Materials are prepared and gathered in advance of the lesson. Routines for effectively using a variety of tools/media and handling materials and supplies are seamless, with students assuming some responsibility for smooth operation.
2c3
Performance of Non-Instructional Duties
Any non-instructional duties are performed while students are engaged in productive work. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Efficient systems for performing non-instructional duties are in place, resulting in minimal loss of instructional time.
Attendance, lunch counts, and other administrative tasks are handled quickly and without halting instruction.
At the Highly Effective level: Systems for non-instructional duties are well established, with students assuming considerable responsibility for efficient operation.
No evidence for Performance of Non-Instructional Duties observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Taking attendance digitally or via a quick visual scan while students are engaged in a warm-up or do-now; using systems that do not require stopping instruction; completing administrative tasks unobtrusively while students work independently or in groups. Remaining productively engaged in a task while the teacher completes administrative duties; at the Highly Effective level, students support the efficiency of non-instructional routines so that instructional time is fully protected.
Ineffective
Considerable instructional time is lost in performing non-instructional duties.
Developing
Systems for performing non-instructional duties are only fairly efficient, resulting in some loss of instructional time.
Effective
Efficient systems for performing non-instructional duties are in place, resulting in minimal loss of instructional time.
Highly Effective
Systems for performing non-instructional duties are well established, with students assuming considerable responsibility for efficient operation.
2c4
Management of Parent Leaders, Other Volunteers and Paraprofessionals
Volunteers and/or paraprofessionals have clear roles that promote student learning. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO). EDSNCT Focus Element
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Teacher provides parent leaders, volunteers, and/or paraprofessionals with clear direction regarding tasks.
Support personnel are productively and independently engaged during the entire class.
At the Highly Effective level: Teacher provides direction to support personnel in advance of the class. Support staff are productively and independently engaged throughout and make a substantive contribution to the classroom environment.
No evidence for Management of Volunteers and Paraprofessionals observed.
EDSNCT Focus Element — Management of Parent Leaders, other Volunteers and Paraprofessionals (2c4) is a focus element for the EDSNCT evaluation cycle.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Briefing paraprofessionals or volunteers before class on their specific role and assigned students or groups; providing written instructions or reference materials so support personnel can work independently; checking in briefly without halting instruction; using support personnel strategically to extend reach to more students. Receiving targeted support from paraprofessionals or volunteers that extends what the teacher is able to provide; working productively with support personnel who have clear roles and are effectively integrated into the lesson's flow.
Ineffective
Parent leaders, volunteers, and/or paraprofessionals have few clearly defined duties and are idle most of the time.
Developing
Parent leaders, volunteers, and/or paraprofessionals are productively engaged during portions of class time but require frequent direction from teacher.
Effective
Teacher provides parent leaders, volunteers, and/or paraprofessionals with clear direction regarding tasks and they are productively and independently engaged during the entire class.
Highly Effective
Teacher provides parent leaders, volunteers, and/or paraprofessionals with clear direction regarding tasks in advance of the class; they are productively and independently engaged during the entire class and make a substantive contribution to the classroom environment.

Component 2d: Managing Student Behavior

A key to efficient and respectful management of student behavior lies in agreed-upon standards of conduct and clear consequences for overstepping boundaries. Effective teachers successfully enlist students in both setting and maintaining standards of conduct. In a well-managed classroom, students themselves will be able to explain, uphold, and self-manage the agreed-upon standards of conduct.

2d1
Expectations for Behavior
Expectations for behavior are clear to all students and parents. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Standards of conduct are clear to all students and parents.
Teacher always models expectations for self-management and social awareness.
At the Highly Effective level: Standards of conduct appear to have been developed with student participation. Both teacher and students always model expectations, self-management, and social awareness.
No evidence for Expectations for Behavior observed.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Posting and referencing behavioral expectations consistently; modeling self-management and social awareness through their own conduct; reminding students of expectations proactively rather than reactively; establishing norms collaboratively (at the Highly Effective level); communicating expectations to families. Demonstrating awareness of behavioral expectations without needing reminders; self-correcting when they drift from expectations; at the Highly Effective level, students model expectations for one another and take shared ownership of the behavioral norms they helped create.
Ineffective
No standards of conduct appear to have been established, or students are confused as to what the standards are. Teacher does not model expectations, self-management, or social awareness.
Developing
Standards of conduct appear to have been established, and most students seem to understand them. Teacher usually models expectations, self-management, or social awareness.
Effective
Standards of conduct are clear to all students and parents. Teacher always models expectations for self-management and social awareness.
Highly Effective
Standards of conduct are clear to all students and parents and appear to have been developed with student participation. Teacher and students always model expectations, self-management, and social awareness.
2d2
Monitoring and Responding to Student Behavior
The teacher monitors and responds to behaviors in a way that maintains student dignity and does not disrupt learning. Evidence source: Classroom Observation (CO).
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Student behavior generally supports learning.
Teacher reinforces positive behaviors when appropriate.
If misbehavior occurs, teacher has a clear and consistent system for addressing negative behavior that respects the student's dignity.
Teacher's response to misbehavior produces desired results.
At the Highly Effective level: Student behavior supports learning. Teacher's response to misbehavior is sensitive to students' individual needs. Students monitor their own and their peers' behavior.
No evidence for Monitoring and Responding to Student Behavior observed.
EDST Focus Element — Monitoring and Responding to Student Behavior (2d2) is a focus element for the EDST evaluation cycle. Effective teachers address behavior without shaming students, without halting instruction unnecessarily, and with consistency across all students.
What is the teacher doing?What are the students doing?
Using proximity, non-verbal cues, and private redirects to address behavior without disrupting instruction; acknowledging positive behavior specifically and publicly when appropriate; responding to misbehavior consistently and privately; maintaining student dignity in all interactions; applying a tiered, predictable response system. Self-regulating behavior in response to teacher cues without the lesson stopping; returning to productive work quickly after redirection; at the Highly Effective level, students monitor their own behavior and support peers in maintaining the learning environment — demonstrating internalized behavioral standards.
Ineffective
Student behavior hinders learning. Teacher does not monitor student behavior, or teacher responds in a way that does not produce desirable results. Appropriate behaviors are not acknowledged. Teacher does not respond to misbehavior, or the response is inconsistent, overly repressive, or does not respect the student's dignity.
Developing
Some student behavior may hinder learning. Teacher is generally aware of student behavior and occasionally acknowledges appropriate behaviors. Teacher attempts to respond to student misbehavior but with uneven results.
Effective
Student behavior generally supports learning. Teacher reinforces positive behaviors when appropriate. If misbehavior occurs, teacher has a clear and consistent system for addressing negative behavior or rule-breaking that respects the student's dignity. Teacher's response to misbehavior produces desired results.
Highly Effective
Student behavior supports learning. Teacher reinforces positive behaviors when appropriate. Teacher's response to misbehavior is sensitive to students' individual needs. Students monitor their own and their peers' behavior.
