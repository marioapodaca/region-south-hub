LAUSD Teaching and Learning Framework — Standard 3: Delivery of Instruction

This reference page contains the five TLF rubric categories included on the Region South Informal Classroom Observation Form, along with their full look-fors, performance level descriptors, and evidence guidance. It is intended for use by all Region South leadership roles during and after Monday school visits.

📋 Region South Informal Classroom Observation Form — 5 rubric categories: 3c1 · 3b2 · 3a4 · 3c2 · 3d3

Standard 3: Delivery of Instruction

The five elements below are the focus of the Region South Informal Classroom Observation Form. Each element includes look-fors, performance level descriptors (Ineffective → Developing → Effective → Highly Effective), and a two-column evidence structure matching the observation form (What is the teacher doing? / What are the students doing?).

3c1
Standards-Based Projects, Activities, and Assignments
Component 3c: Structures to Engage Students in Learning — Standards-aligned learning activities cognitively engage students in the lesson.
On Observation Form
Look-Fors — What observers check on the form
Assignments and activities are aligned with grade-level standards and are designed to promote higher-order thinking skills.
Core curriculum lesson look-fors are evident.
Assignments and activities are culturally and linguistically relevant; may include real-world applications.
Students are constructing their own understanding and exploring content.
Learning activities are differentiated as appropriate.
Evidence of rigorous, grade-level activities (Yes / No checkbox on form).
No evidence for Standards-Based Projects, Activities, and Assignments observed.
EDST Focus Element and EDSNCT Focus Element — This element is a unified focus element for both classroom teacher and non-classroom teacher evaluation cycles.
What is the teacher doing? What are the students doing?
Delivering instruction tied to grade-level content standards with explicit connections to learning objectives. Selecting tasks that require students to apply, analyze, evaluate, or create rather than recall. Incorporating culturally relevant contexts and real-world applications. Differentiating activities for student subgroups. Engaging in tasks that require higher-order thinking — not passive receiving. Constructing their own understanding through inquiry, problem-solving, writing, or investigation. Applying content in contexts connected to their lives and experiences.
Ineffective
Projects, activities, and assignments do not require higher levels of thinking or are not aligned to instructional standards. Few or no learners are cognitively engaged.
Developing
Some projects, activities, and assignments are aligned to standards and may require higher-level thinking. Some learners are cognitively engaged. Activities are differentiated to meet the needs of some students.
Effective
Instructional projects, activities, and assignments are aligned to standards, require higher levels of thinking, are culturally relevant, and may include real-world application. Most learners are cognitively engaged, constructing their own understanding and exploring content. Activities are differentiated for represented subgroups.
Highly Effective
All of the above, with learners cognitively engaged throughout. Teacher encourages and supports learners in initiating or adapting activities to enhance their understanding. Activities are differentiated to meet all learning needs.
3b2
Discussion Techniques and Student Participation
Component 3b: Using Questioning and Discussion Techniques — Techniques are used to ensure that all students share their thinking around challenging questions.
On Observation Form
Look-Fors — What observers check on the form
Teacher uses intentional, differentiated strategies to engage all students in discussion.
Evidence of teachers and/or students using higher DOK rigorous questioning.
Teacher works to gradually shift from teacher-directed to student-directed conversations.
Students participate in challenging discussions.
Strategies affirm the students' culture and language (CLRP).
No evidence for Discussion Techniques and Student Participation observed.
EDST Focus Element and EDSNCT Focus Element — This element is a unified focus element for both evaluation cycles. Also directly connected to 3b1 (Quality and Purpose of Questions) which is an EDST Focus Element.
What is the teacher doing? What are the students doing?
Using intentional discussion strategies (think-pair-share, Socratic seminar, numbered heads, accountable talk stems, fishbowl, debate). Posing questions at DOK 3–4 level. Deliberately shifting talk from teacher-to-student to student-to-student. Culturally affirming student contributions and language backgrounds. Sharing their own thinking — not just answering teacher questions. Building on each other's ideas. Using academic language in discussion. Engaging in intellectually challenging dialogue. Asking their own questions to advance understanding.
Ineffective
Teacher makes no attempt to differentiate discussion. Teacher generates the majority of questions and most answers. Little student-to-student talk.
Developing
Teacher makes some attempt to use differentiated strategies with uneven results. Only some learners participate. Discussion is not intellectually challenging throughout.
Effective
Teacher uses intentional, differentiated strategies to engage all learners in discussion. Gradual release from teacher-directed to learner-initiated conversation. Students participate in intellectually challenging discussions.
Highly Effective
Teacher uses intentional strategies to engage all learners in intellectually challenging learner-to-learner discussions. Teacher creates conditions for learners to assume considerable responsibility for the success of the discussions.
3a4
Use of Academic Language
Component 3a: Communicating with Students — Academic language is used to communicate and deepen understanding of the content.
On Observation Form
Look-Fors — What observers check on the form
Teacher models correct use of academic language.
Teacher provides students structured opportunities to understand academic language.
Teacher provides students structured opportunities to use academic language in speaking and writing.
Teacher connects the academic language to the students' culture and language (CLRP).
Students comprehend and use academic language in listening, speaking, reading, and writing.
No evidence for the Use of Academic Language look-fors observed.
EDST Focus Element — This element is a focus element for the classroom teacher evaluation cycle. Academic language includes both general academic vocabulary (Tier 2) and discipline-specific vocabulary (Tier 3).
What is the teacher doing? What are the students doing?
Explicitly modeling correct use of academic vocabulary in context. Providing structured opportunities for students to practice using academic language — sentence frames, word walls, academic language routines. Connecting new vocabulary to students' home language or cultural context. Scaffolding for English Learners without lowering cognitive demand. Using academic vocabulary accurately in discussion and writing. Demonstrating comprehension of discipline-specific terms. Moving beyond everyday language to express ideas with precision. English Learners approximating academic language with scaffolded support.
Academic Language — Two Types Observers Should Distinguish
Tier 2 — General Academic: Words used across disciplines that students need for school success.
analyzeevaluatecomparesummarizejustifyevidenceargumentperspective
Tier 3 — Domain Specific: Content vocabulary unique to the subject being taught.
photosynthesisequationprotagonistmitosisdenominatorfigurative languagehypothesis
Ineffective
Teacher and students rarely or incorrectly use academic language. No structured opportunities for students to practice.
Developing
Teacher inconsistently uses academic language. Limited opportunities for students to comprehend and incorporate academic language across listening, speaking, reading, and writing.
Effective
Teacher models correct use of academic language. Teacher scaffolds student learning by providing structured opportunities for subgroups of students to comprehend and use academic language in listening, speaking, reading, and writing.
Highly Effective
Teacher correctly and consistently uses academic language. Teacher scaffolds student learning, only as needed, providing structured opportunities to ensure that ALL students comprehend and use academic language across all four domains.
3c2
Purposeful and Productive Groups
Component 3c: Structures to Engage Students in Learning — Students are grouped in order to promote productive cognitive engagement in the lesson.
On Observation Form
Look-Fors — What observers check on the form
Teacher uses instructional groups that support the outcome(s) of the lesson.
Students are cognitively engaged and work collaboratively in their groups.
Group structures and tasks are differentiated as necessary.
Group assignments and structures reflect awareness of students' language and culture.
No evidence for Purposeful and Productive Groups look-fors observed.
EDST Focus Element — This element is a focus element for the classroom teacher evaluation cycle. Groups must be purposefully designed around the lesson's learning outcome — not simply seating arrangements or convenience groupings.
What is the teacher doing? What are the students doing?
Designing group structures intentionally based on the learning outcome — not defaulting to permanent seat groups. Assigning roles or tasks that require each member's contribution. Differentiating group tasks based on student readiness, language level, or learning need. Grouping in ways that reflect cultural awareness and student strengths. Working purposefully and productively — all group members cognitively engaged, not one student doing the work. Sharing responsibility for group outcomes. Collaborating to construct understanding rather than dividing and completing tasks independently.
Ineffective
Instructional group structures do not support student learning toward the instructional outcomes of the lesson.
Developing
Group structures support some students. Some students are cognitively engaged and work purposefully. Group structures, tasks, or products are not differentiated when needed.
Effective
Group structures support most students in achieving instructional outcomes. Students are cognitively engaged and work purposefully and productively. Group structures, tasks, or products may be differentiated for student subgroups.
Highly Effective
Group structures support ALL students. Students are cognitively engaged and share responsibility for achieving outcomes. Group structures, tasks, or products are differentiated to meet the needs of all students.
3d3
Feedback to Students
Component 3d: Using Assessment in Instruction to Advance Student Learning — Students receive specific and timely feedback that will move their learning forward.
On Observation Form
Look-Fors — What observers check on the form
Teacher provides timely, frequent, relevant feedback.
Feedback is accurate and aligned to instructional outcome(s).
Teacher provides specific feedback that helps students to improve their work.
No evidence of Feedback to Students observed.
EDST Focus Element and EDSNCT Focus Element — This element is a unified focus element for both evaluation cycles. Feedback must be timely, accurate, specific, and aligned to the instructional outcome — not general praise or correction.
What is the teacher doing? What are the students doing?
Providing feedback that is specific to the learning objective — not general ("good job") but targeted ("your claim is clear, but you need one more piece of textual evidence to support it"). Giving feedback while students are still working, not only at the end. Aligning feedback to the success criteria students already know. Fostering growth mindset through feedback that names effort and specific next steps. Receiving and acting on feedback — revising work, adjusting thinking, or trying a different approach. At Highly Effective level: students provide each other with actionable feedback and demonstrate self-efficacy by using feedback to improve independently.
Ineffective
Teacher feedback is non-existent, limited, infrequent, and/or inaccurate. Feedback is not aligned to the instructional outcome nor does it foster self-efficacy or a growth mindset.
Developing
Feedback may be consistent, timely, and/or accurate, but is limited to praise or correction. Feedback may not be aligned to the instructional outcome or serve to foster self-efficacy or a growth mindset.
Effective
Feedback is timely, frequent, relevant, accurate, and aligned to the instructional outcome. Specific feedback guides learners to revise and improve their work, fostering self-efficacy and a growth mindset.
Highly Effective
All of the above, with learners demonstrating self-efficacy and a growth mindset by making use of specific feedback to revise their work. Learners work collaboratively with peers to provide each other with actionable feedback.
