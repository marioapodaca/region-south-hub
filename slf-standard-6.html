SLF Standard 6: Systems and Operations

Standard 6 addresses the operational leadership of the school — the systems, structures, and compliance frameworks that create the conditions under which instruction can occur at the highest level. While Standard 6 is sometimes seen as the most "administrative" of the six standards, it is deeply connected to instructional leadership: a school with chaotic systems, unsafe facilities, inequitable resource allocation, or chronic legal compliance failures cannot sustain the instructional improvement described in Standards 1 through 5.

Three components organize this standard: 6A (Manages people, time, and resources), 6B (Creates or maintains a productive school environment), and 6C (Demonstrates legal and policy compliance). Component 6A connects directly to the time management and resource prioritization elements in Standard 1 (1A.2). Component 6B describes the physical and operational conditions for learning. Component 6C is a baseline requirement — Ineffective practice here can have legal, ethical, and safety consequences that override growth in all other areas.

Component 6A: Manages People, Time, and Resources

This component addresses how principals allocate the school's most finite assets — staff time, budget, facilities, and personnel — in ways that maximize their impact on student learning. Effective resource management is not primarily a financial skill; it is a strategic and equity skill. Who gets which resources, how time is protected for instruction, and how personnel decisions are made all reflect and shape the school's values.

6A.1
Manages, Evaluates, and Develops Human Resources
Makes strategic, equitable, and student-centered decisions about hiring, assignment, and deployment of staff; builds personnel capacity through ongoing evaluation, coaching, and development; manages personnel challenges promptly and fairly.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Makes strategic, equitable, and student-centered decisions about hiring, assignment, and deployment of instructional and support staff.
Builds personnel capacity through the ongoing evaluation process and targeted professional development.
Manages personnel challenges — including chronic absenteeism, performance concerns, and interpersonal conflicts — promptly, fairly, and in compliance with district procedures.
At the Highly Effective level: Systematically uses data on student needs and staff strengths to make strategic staffing decisions; proactively identifies and addresses performance concerns before they escalate; builds a pipeline of teacher leaders ready to assume expanded responsibilities.
No evidence for Managing, Evaluating, and Developing Human Resources observed.
Connection to Standard 3: The quality of personnel decisions (6A.1) shapes the conditions in which coaching (3C.2) and evaluation (3C.3) occur. A principal who makes strategic, student-centered staffing decisions — assigning the strongest teachers to the highest-need students, deploying paraprofessionals with intentionality, and addressing chronic staff absenteeism swiftly — is building the human infrastructure that instructional leadership requires.
Examples of Evidence
Master schedule demonstrating strategic teacher assignment by student need and content expertise
Hiring rubrics and interview documentation reflecting student-centered priorities
Substitute utilization data and management plan for chronic staff absenteeism
Documentation of personnel actions taken within required timelines
Staff deployment plans showing paraprofessional and support staff roles tied to instructional priorities
MyPGS completion rates and timeliness for all evaluation cycles
Ineffective
Makes personnel decisions without regard to student need or staff strengths; fails to address personnel challenges promptly or in compliance with district procedures; evaluation process is incomplete, inaccurate, or used punitively rather than developmentally.
Developing
Makes some student-centered personnel decisions but inconsistently; addresses personnel challenges when they escalate but not proactively; uses the evaluation process primarily for compliance rather than staff development.
Effective
Makes strategic, equitable, and student-centered decisions about hiring, assignment, and deployment of staff; builds personnel capacity through the ongoing evaluation process and targeted professional development; manages personnel challenges promptly, fairly, and in compliance with district procedures.
Highly Effective
Systematically uses data on student needs and staff strengths to make strategic staffing decisions; proactively identifies and addresses performance concerns and personnel challenges before they escalate; builds a pipeline of teacher leaders and systematically develops staff capacity at all levels of the organization.
6A.2
Manages Time to Maximize Learning
Protects and maximizes instructional time; designs schedules and school routines that minimize time lost to non-instructional activities; ensures equitable access to instructional time across student groups.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Protects instructional time as the school's highest-priority resource — interruptions, announcements, and pull-outs are minimized and managed.
Master schedule is designed to maximize time on task for all students, including equitable access to grade-level content for ELs and students with disabilities.
Transitions, routines, and school-wide procedures are efficient and taught explicitly so they do not consume instructional time.
At the Highly Effective level: Time analysis data is used to identify and address systemic time losses; schedule is regularly reviewed and revised based on student outcome data; all student groups have equitable and maximized access to instructional time.
No evidence for Managing Time to Maximize Learning observed.
Site visit connection: Time lost to transitions, late starts, intercom interruptions, and off-task activity during leadership classroom visits is direct evidence for both the teacher's 2c1 (management of routines and transitions) and the principal's 6A.2. A school where instructional minutes are routinely lost to preventable inefficiencies reflects a principal who has not yet established time as a protected resource.
Examples of Evidence
Master schedule reviewed for instructional time alignment by student group
Data on pull-out frequency and impact on EL and SWD instructional time
Bell-to-bell instruction data from informal observations
School-wide routines documented and taught explicitly at year start
Comparison of planned vs. actual instructional minutes (ADA audit data)
Schedule revision history showing response to student outcome data
Ineffective
Does not protect instructional time; the schedule contains significant structural time losses; interruptions, pull-outs, and non-instructional activities regularly consume teaching and learning time; some student groups have significantly less access to instructional time than others.
Developing
Makes some effort to protect instructional time but inconsistently; the schedule has some structural inefficiencies; some student groups may have less access to instructional time.
Effective
Protects and maximizes instructional time as a priority resource; designs schedules and routines that minimize time lost to non-instructional activities; ensures equitable access to instructional time across student groups including ELs and students with disabilities.
Highly Effective
Uses time analysis data to identify and eliminate systemic time losses; regularly reviews and revises the master schedule based on student outcome data; all student groups have equitable and maximized access to instructional time; the school culture treats every instructional minute as valuable.
6A.3
Manages Fiscal and Material Resources Equitably and Strategically
Allocates fiscal and material resources — including Title I funds, categorical funding, instructional materials, and technology — in alignment with school improvement priorities and in ways that address the needs of underserved student populations first.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Allocates fiscal and material resources in alignment with school improvement priorities and student needs.
Resource allocation decisions are transparent, equitable, and data-informed — underserved student populations receive resources proportionate to their needs.
Manages all fiscal resources in compliance with district, state, and federal requirements.
At the Highly Effective level: Collaboratively develops resource allocation plans with stakeholder input; regularly audits resource distribution to verify equity; leverages grant funding and community partnerships to expand the school's resource base.
No evidence for Managing Fiscal and Material Resources Equitably and Strategically observed.
Examples of Evidence
SPSA budget aligned to identified student needs and school improvement goals
Title I expenditure documentation showing alignment to high-need students
Instructional materials inventory showing equitable distribution across grade levels and classrooms
Technology access audit — do all students have equitable device and connectivity access?
School budget presented to SSC with opportunity for family input
Impress fund, Donation fund, and P-card expenditure documentation
Grant applications and community partnership resources aligned to school priorities
Ineffective
Allocates resources without reference to student needs or school improvement priorities; resource distribution is inequitable or non-compliant with district, state, or federal requirements; fiscal records are incomplete or inaccurate.
Developing
Makes some effort to align resource allocation to school priorities but inconsistently; resource distribution may not fully address the needs of underserved student populations; fiscal management is generally compliant but with some gaps.
Effective
Allocates fiscal and material resources in alignment with school improvement priorities and student needs; resource allocation decisions are transparent, equitable, and data-informed; manages all fiscal resources in compliance with district, state, and federal requirements.
Highly Effective
Collaboratively develops resource allocation plans with stakeholder input; regularly audits resource distribution to verify equity; leverages grant funding and community partnerships to expand the school's resource base; resource allocation is recognized as a tool for equity and school improvement.

Component 6B: Creates or Maintains a Productive School Environment

This component addresses the physical and operational conditions that make effective learning possible — safe facilities, efficient operations, and a school environment that communicates to students, staff, and families that this is a place where learning matters. A well-maintained, organized, and welcoming school environment is not a luxury; it is a communication about values and expectations.

6B.1
Creates and Maintains a Safe and Supportive School Environment
Establishes and maintains physical and emotional safety as a non-negotiable foundation for learning; implements comprehensive safety systems including emergency preparedness, facilities maintenance, and proactive identification and response to safety concerns.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Establishes and maintains physical safety systems — facilities are well-maintained, hazards are addressed promptly, and emergency preparedness plans are current and practiced.
Establishes and maintains emotional safety — students and staff report feeling safe at school and incidents of bullying, harassment, or discrimination are addressed swiftly and thoroughly.
Safety concerns are proactively identified and addressed before they escalate.
At the Highly Effective level: Safety systems are comprehensive, data-driven, and continuously improved; the school community co-owns safety as a shared responsibility; all students and staff consistently report high levels of physical and emotional safety.
No evidence for Creating and Maintaining a Safe and Supportive School Environment observed.
Examples of Evidence
School Safety Plan — current, practiced, and accessible to all staff
Facilities maintenance logs and work order completion rates
Monthly safety drills documented (fire, earthquake, lockdown)
Student and staff safety survey data (School Experience Survey)
Bullying, harassment, and discrimination incident log with documented follow-up
Physical plant inspection records showing timely remediation of hazards
Safety committee meeting minutes and action items
Ineffective
Does not maintain physical safety systems or does not respond to safety concerns promptly; the school environment contains unaddressed physical hazards or persistent emotional safety issues including bullying, harassment, or discrimination; emergency preparedness systems are outdated or untested.
Developing
Maintains basic physical safety systems and responds to safety concerns when they escalate; some students or staff may not feel consistently safe at school; emergency preparedness plans exist but may not be current or regularly practiced.
Effective
Establishes and maintains physical and emotional safety systems; facilities are well-maintained and hazards are addressed promptly; bullying, harassment, and discrimination are addressed swiftly; emergency preparedness plans are current and practiced; safety concerns are proactively identified and addressed before they escalate.
Highly Effective
Safety systems are comprehensive, data-driven, and continuously improved based on incident data and climate surveys; the school community co-owns safety as a shared responsibility; all students and staff consistently report high levels of physical and emotional safety; the school environment communicates that every person's safety is valued.
6B.2
Manages School Operations Efficiently and Effectively
Establishes efficient systems for school-wide operations — including office procedures, student arrival and dismissal, substitute management, visitor protocols, and daily logistics — so that operational demands do not consume instructional leadership capacity.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Establishes clear, documented systems for routine school operations that run efficiently without requiring constant principal attention.
Office and support staff are well-trained, clearly assigned, and operate with defined procedures that welcome and serve all families equitably.
Operational systems free the principal to focus on instructional leadership rather than daily firefighting.
At the Highly Effective level: Operational systems are regularly audited and improved; staff take ownership of operational functions; the school runs smoothly even in the principal's absence; visitors and families consistently report positive, efficient, and welcoming experiences.
No evidence for Managing School Operations Efficiently and Effectively observed.
Connection to Standard 1 (1A.2): A principal who is consumed by daily operational problems — managing substitute coverage, resolving parent complaints at the front office, responding to facilities requests — has not yet built the operational systems that would free them to prioritize instructional leadership. Effective operational management is what creates the time for Standard 1 and Standard 3 work.
Examples of Evidence
Documented office procedures for visitor check-in, phone protocols, and family reception
Substitute management system — coverage rates and instructional continuity plans
Student arrival and dismissal protocols documented and practiced
Operations manual or staff handbook with clear role assignments
Evidence that school runs smoothly during principal absence (assistant principal logs, observation notes)
Parent satisfaction with office and front-of-school experience (survey data)
Ineffective
School operations are chaotic or inefficient; routine operational problems consume disproportionate principal time; office and support staff lack clear procedures or training; families and visitors report frustrating or unwelcoming experiences.
Developing
Some operational systems are in place but inconsistently implemented; operational demands occasionally pull the principal away from instructional leadership priorities; office and support staff have some training but may not have clear, documented procedures.
Effective
Establishes clear, efficient systems for school-wide operations that run without constant principal attention; office and support staff are well-trained with defined procedures that welcome and serve all families equitably; operational systems free the principal to prioritize instructional leadership.
Highly Effective
Operational systems are regularly audited and improved based on data and staff feedback; staff take ownership of operational functions; the school runs smoothly even in the principal's absence; visitors and families consistently report positive, efficient, and welcoming experiences.
6B.3
Maintains a Physical Environment Conducive to Learning
Ensures that the school's physical environment — including classrooms, common areas, and outdoor spaces — is clean, well-maintained, organized, and communicates high expectations for learning through its appearance and use.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Classrooms, hallways, common areas, and outdoor spaces are clean, well-maintained, and organized.
The physical environment communicates high expectations — student work is displayed with pride, instructional spaces are set up for learning, and the school looks and feels like a place where learning matters.
Facilities concerns are addressed promptly through effective use of custodial staff and district maintenance systems.
At the Highly Effective level: The physical environment is actively used as a learning tool — it reflects student identity, celebrates achievement, and communicates the school's values and priorities to all who enter.
No evidence for Maintaining a Physical Environment Conducive to Learning observed.
Examples of Evidence
Physical plant inspection scores and remediation timelines
Custodial staff deployment plan aligned to high-traffic and high-need areas
Student work displayed throughout school reflecting current learning
Classroom setup observed during site visits — furniture arrangement, learning materials, student work
Family and visitor first impressions — cleanliness, signage, welcoming displays
Evidence of garden, mural, or other community-built spaces that reflect student identity
Ineffective
The school's physical environment is poorly maintained, disorganized, or communicates low expectations; facilities concerns go unaddressed; classrooms and common areas do not reflect student learning or pride in the school.
Developing
The school's physical environment is generally clean but inconsistently maintained; some facilities concerns are addressed promptly; the physical environment partially communicates learning expectations through some student work displays.
Effective
The school's physical environment is clean, well-maintained, and organized; the environment communicates high expectations through student work displays and learning-focused classroom setups; facilities concerns are addressed promptly through effective use of custodial and maintenance systems.
Highly Effective
The physical environment is actively and intentionally used as a learning tool that reflects student identity, celebrates achievement, and communicates the school's values; the school looks and feels like a place where learning is the highest priority; all stakeholders report pride in and ownership of the school's physical environment.

Component 6C: Demonstrates Legal and Policy Compliance

This component addresses the principal's responsibility to know and follow the laws, policies, and regulations that govern public school operations — including those protecting students' civil rights, special education rights, language access rights, and due process rights. Compliance is not the ceiling of professional practice; it is the floor. An Effective principal does not merely comply — they build a school culture in which compliance is understood by all staff as an expression of their commitment to students' rights and dignity.

6C.1
Knows and Follows Laws, Policies, and Regulations
Demonstrates working knowledge of and consistent compliance with relevant federal, state, and district laws, policies, and regulations — including those governing special education, English Learner rights, civil rights, mandated reporting, due process, and fiscal management.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Demonstrates working knowledge of laws, policies, and regulations governing the school's operation — including IDEA, Title III, Section 504, FERPA, AB 1825, and LAUSD policies.
Consistently administers school programs in compliance with applicable legal requirements.
Identifies and addresses compliance gaps proactively — does not wait for audit findings or parent complaints to discover non-compliance.
At the Highly Effective level: Builds staff knowledge of legal requirements so that compliance is distributed and not dependent solely on the principal; proactively trains staff on rights of students and families; legal compliance is understood as an expression of the school's equity commitments.
No evidence for Knowing and Following Laws, Policies, and Regulations observed.
Examples of Evidence
On-time IEP completion rates — all IEPs held within required timelines
English Learner reclassification data — process followed with fidelity and family notification in required language
FERPA compliance — student records access and release handled correctly
Section 504 plans current and implemented with fidelity
Mandated reporter training completion for all staff
Compliance audit results and timely remediation of findings
Staff trained on legal requirements for EL services, special education, and civil rights
Ineffective
Demonstrates limited knowledge of or fails to comply with federal, state, or district laws and regulations; compliance gaps result in violations of student or family rights; non-compliance is reactive — discovered through audits, complaints, or legal action rather than proactive monitoring.
Developing
Demonstrates basic knowledge of applicable laws and generally complies, but with some gaps; compliance is primarily reactive — concerns are addressed when identified by audits or complaints rather than through proactive monitoring; staff may not have sufficient knowledge of their legal obligations.
Effective
Demonstrates working knowledge of laws, policies, and regulations; consistently administers school programs in compliance with applicable legal requirements; identifies and addresses compliance gaps proactively before audit findings or parent complaints surface them.
Highly Effective
Builds distributed staff knowledge of legal requirements so that compliance is not dependent on the principal alone; proactively trains staff on the rights of students and families; legal compliance is understood and practiced as an expression of the school's equity commitments; the school has no significant compliance findings across evaluation cycles.
6C.2
Ensures Equitable Access and Opportunity for All Students
Actively ensures that all students — including English Learners, students with disabilities, students experiencing homelessness, foster youth, and other protected classes — have equitable access to all programs, services, and opportunities the school offers, as required by law and as a matter of educational equity.
Look-Fors — Evidence of Effective Practice
Actively monitors and ensures equitable access to all school programs — including gifted programs, extracurriculars, dual enrollment, AP, and accelerated coursework — for all student populations.
English Learners receive required language services and are on track for reclassification.
Students with disabilities are educated in the least restrictive environment appropriate to their needs, with IEPs implemented with fidelity.
Foster youth and students experiencing homelessness receive required supports and immediate enrollment without barriers.
At the Highly Effective level: The principal proactively dismantles systemic barriers to access; all student groups are proportionately represented in rigorous and enrichment programs; access data is regularly reviewed and shared with the school community.
No evidence for Ensuring Equitable Access and Opportunity for All Students observed.
Region South connection: This element intersects directly with Region South's A-G completion tracking and GATE equity work. A principal who monitors whether ELs and students with disabilities are enrolled in A-G courses at rates comparable to their peers — and takes action when they are not — is demonstrating Effective or Highly Effective practice on 6C.2. The Region South School Data Dashboard is a direct tool for this element.
Examples of Evidence
A-G enrollment data disaggregated by EL status, disability status, and race/ethnicity
GATE/gifted identification rates by subgroup compared to school population demographics
LRE (Least Restrictive Environment) data for students with disabilities
EL service delivery documentation — all ELs receiving required instructional services
McKinney-Vento compliance documentation for students experiencing homelessness
Foster youth liaison documentation and support plan completion rates
Extracurricular and enrichment participation data by student subgroup
Ineffective
Does not actively monitor or ensure equitable access; systemic barriers to access for protected student groups exist and go unaddressed; EL services, IEP implementation, or programs for foster youth and students experiencing homelessness are incomplete or non-compliant.
Developing
Makes some effort to ensure equitable access but inconsistently; some student groups face barriers to programs or services that are identified but not systematically addressed; EL and special education compliance is generally maintained but with gaps.
Effective
Actively monitors and ensures equitable access to all school programs for all student populations; ELs receive required language services; students with disabilities are educated in the LRE with IEPs implemented with fidelity; foster youth and students experiencing homelessness receive required supports without enrollment barriers.
Highly Effective
Proactively dismantles systemic barriers to access; all student groups are proportionately represented in rigorous and enrichment programs; access data is regularly reviewed and shared with the school community as an equity accountability measure; the school is recognized as a model for equitable access and opportunity.
